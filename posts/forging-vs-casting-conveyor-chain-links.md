A conveyor chain in a cement plant, a mine or a bulk-handling line takes shock loads all day, in dust and heat. When a link fails, the line stops. So the first question a buyer asks about a new chain part is simple: should it be forged or cast?

## What forging does to the steel

In hot forging, a heated steel billet is pressed or hammered into shape. The metal is not melted. It is squeezed, and its grain follows the shape of the part instead of being cut across. Forging also closes up internal voids that can be present in the bar.

For a part that is pulled, bent and struck in service, that matters. A forged link usually has:

- **Better toughness under impact**, because the grain runs along the load path.
- **More consistent strength from piece to piece**, because there is no shrinkage porosity of the kind casting can leave.
- **Better fatigue life**, which is what wears out a chain that runs around sprockets thousands of times a day.

## Where casting still fits

Casting pours molten metal into a mould. It is a good choice for large, complex or hollow shapes that cannot be forged in one piece, and for low-stress housings and covers. For the load-bearing members of a heavy chain, such as block links, drag and scraper links, attachment links and side bars, forging is the usual choice.

## What decides it for your part

1. **How the part is loaded.** Shock and repeated loading point to forging.
2. **The shape.** Solid, compact shapes forge well. Deep hollow shapes may need casting or machining from a forging.
3. **The quantity.** Forging dies cost money up front, so they pay back best on repeat orders.
4. **The finish you need.** Forged parts are usually machined on critical faces and bores afterwards.
